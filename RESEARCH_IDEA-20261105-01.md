> Scope: this dossier researches the perceptual phenomenon in which an ambiguous two-tone image becomes recognizable after exposure to its corresponding intact image. It does not claim that the physical image changes, that all viewers experience the same effect, or that one exposure always produces recognition.

## 1. Research question and editorial framing

**Core question:** How can a brief visual cue change the way a person recognizes the exact same ambiguous image?

**Working What If question:** “Bagaimana jika satu petunjuk membuat otakmu melihat gambar yang sama dengan cara berbeda?”

**Recommended scientific framing:** a documented perceptual-learning phenomenon, demonstrated through Mooney/two-tone images. The “What If” framing is a viewer-facing question, not a fictional medical scenario.

**Central distinction:** the stimulus can remain physically identical while recognition and the brain's neural response to it change after the matching intact image has supplied useful information.

**Do not frame as:** “the brain changes reality,” “the eyes suddenly see new pixels,” “one glance rewires the brain permanently,” or “the brain hallucinates the hidden object.”

## 2. Scientific model

### 2.1 Stimulus
A Mooney/two-tone image is a degraded, high-contrast black-and-white rendering of an original image. In the 2024 iScience study, source grayscale photographs were smoothed and thresholded so pixel luminance was reduced to black or white, obscuring edges and making object identity difficult to recognize.

### 2.2 Experimental sequence
The relevant experimental pattern is:
1. Show the ambiguous two-tone image before cueing; recognition is often poor.
2. Show the corresponding intact grayscale/original photograph so the object identity becomes available.
3. Present the same two-tone image again.
4. Recognition of that image can improve substantially.

The 2026 Nature Neuroscience study used short image presentations and one or a few learning sequences; the reported mean was 1.8 ± 1 learning sequences. This is not a guarantee that every person recognizes every image after one exposure.

### 2.3 Neural findings
The 2026 study recorded single-neuron activity in medial occipital cortex and medial temporal lobe regions in 13 adult patients with pharmacologically resistant epilepsy. Across 34 sessions, the researchers recorded 1,104 neurons. Learning-related changes were observed in neuronal responses in occipital and medial temporal regions. The authors discuss recurrent processing in occipital cortex as a plausible interpretation; this is not a direct causal demonstration of every feedback pathway.

### 2.4 Supporting developmental and memory evidence
- A 2024 iScience study found that the influence of prior knowledge on two-tone image perception develops through childhood. Its adult participants showed perceptual reorganization after the original grayscale cue; the process was not adult-like in the youngest child group.
- A 2021 PNAS study found that one-trial learning of degraded images could improve identification in five patients with medial temporal lobe lesions, even though their explicit recognition memory was impaired. In that specific experiment, perceptual facilitation remained measurable beyond 5.4 months. This is a separate paradigm and must not be merged numerically with the 2026 study.

- Method: two-tone Mooney images and corresponding grayscale images; recognition task; single-neuron recordings in medial occipital and temporal regions; decoding and latency analyses.

## 5. Scientific red-team and exclusions

1. **No magical vision:** the eye does not gain new pixels; the original two-tone stimulus remains the same.
2. **No universal one-look claim:** the 2026 study used one or a few learning sequences (mean 1.8 ± 1); success varied by image and participant.
3. **No population generalization:** intracranial recordings came from 13 patients with drug-resistant epilepsy. The separate healthy eye-tracking cohort was small and is not equivalent to a population-representative neural-recording sample.
4. **No false causality:** neural activity changes and decoding results constrain plausible mechanisms; they do not prove every proposed feedback pathway.
5. **No simplistic “memory switch”:** perceptual learning and explicit remembering are related but distinguishable; the 2021 lesion study is separate evidence and must not be conflated with the 2026 neural-recording experiment.
6. **No medical framing:** this is not a disorder, treatment, diagnostic test, or brain-training prescription.
7. **No universal claim about reality:** do not say “we never see reality as it is” or “the brain invents everything.” The evidence concerns a specific class of ambiguous two-tone images.
8. **No fabricated experiment:** if recreating the phenomenon visually, label it as a demonstration/illustration and use an original stimulus whose source and rights are documented.
9. **No unsourced neuroscience animation:** avoid showing invented neurons physically switching on, synapses rewiring in seconds, or a single brain region “deciding” what the image is.

## 6. Editorial recommendation for later STORY stage

- Strongest narrative contrast: the exact same two-tone image appears before and after the original grayscale cue; the viewer's recognition changes.
- Main mechanism: prior visual information helps interpret an ambiguous stimulus; neuronal response patterns in the cited study changed after learning.
- Human takeaway: what we know can influence how we interpret ambiguous visual information.
- Suggested ending nuance: “Bukan gambarnya yang berubah. Otak kita mendapat petunjuk untuk mengenali pola yang sebelumnya terlewat.”
- Keep the narrative focused on one image pair and one recognition shift. Do not turn it into a broad lecture on memory, hallucination, consciousness, or predictive processing.
